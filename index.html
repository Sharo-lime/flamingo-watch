How you can help

Flamingos need clean, quiet, shallow water. Here are practical ways to help protect it.

Support wetland protection

Donate to or volunteer with groups such as BirdLife International and the IUCN Flamingo Specialist Group, which work to protect key lakes and lagoons.

Donate to BirdLife International →
US donors: Friends of BirdLife International →

Keep your distance

If you see a wild flock, watch from far away, stay out of the water, keep dogs back and never fly a drone near nesting birds.

Cut pollution

Use less single-use plastic, keep chemicals out of drains and choose less fertilizer and pesticide. Runoff ends up in the water flamingos filter.

Reuse and recycle devices

Lithium mining affects Andean flamingo lakes. Keeping phones and laptops longer and recycling batteries properly reduces demand for new mining.

Count what you see

Report flamingo sightings on eBird. Counts from ordinary birdwatchers help scientists track where flocks go.

Visit and support zoos

Accredited zoos like Woodland Park Zoo support conservation work and help people connect with animals they may never see in the wild.

Support Woodland Park Zoo →

Travel responsibly

Choose guides and reserves that follow wildlife viewing rules and respect nesting seasons.

Tell someone

Share this site. People protect what they understand.

Why I made this

Hi, I’m Saul. In 2019 I saw Chilean flamingos at Seattle’s Woodland Park Zoo, and I have loved them ever since.

Chilean flamingos are pale pink birds with black-tipped bills and grey legs with pink joints. Seeing a whole flock standing together made me want to know where they come from and what they need to survive.

What I found is that flamingos can’t simply move somewhere new. They depend on a small number of shallow lakes and lagoons, and many of those places are under pressure. This site is my way of sharing what I learned.

From the zoo to the wild

Woodland Park Zoo’s flock is made up of Chilean flamingos. In 2023 the zoo welcomed Chilean flamingo chicks, the first to hatch there since 2016, from eggs flown in from Zoo Atlanta. The zoo also supports flamingo research and conservation in southern South America.

In the wild, Chilean flamingos live across South America in high-altitude lakes, lowland wetlands and coastal lagoons. The IUCN lists the species as Near Threatened, with a decreasing population. The main causes are egg harvesting, hunting and the loss of wetland habitat.

What I hope you take away

Flamingos are specialists. Each species relies on particular kinds of shallow, mineral-rich water.

Protecting a few places protects a lot of birds. Many flamingos nest at only a handful of sites, so saving one lake can matter for tens of thousands of birds.

Small actions count. Watching flocks from a distance, cutting pollution and supporting conservation groups all help. You can find ideas on the home page.

Threats they face every day

A flamingo cannot simply move on. Across all six species there are only about 30 regularly used breeding sites, so damage to even one lake can affect huge numbers of birds.

Vanishing wetlands

Lagoons are drained or built over for farms, salt works and development. When a lake dries, the algae and tiny shrimp that flamingos eat go with it. Kamfers Dam in South Africa, the only breeding site there, is threatened by pollution and nearby development.

Mining and water use

In the Andes, a 2022 study found that Chile’s Salar de Atacama, the lake closest to lithium mining, lost about 10 to 12 percent of its Andean and James’s flamingos in 11 years, while other lakes stayed steady. In East Africa, proposals to mine soda ash at Lake Natron, where roughly three quarters of the world’s lesser flamingos nest, have worried conservationists for years.

A changing climate

In the high Andes, lakes are shrinking, so there is less food and fewer breeding flamingos. In East Africa the opposite problem appears: a 2024 study found rising water levels are diluting the salty, alkaline soda lakes. Lake Nakuru grew by 91 percent between 2009 and 2022 while its food supply of algae halved.

Pollution and poisoning

Flamingos filter huge amounts of water and mud, so contaminants collect in their bodies. In 1993, polluted water at Lake Bogoria and nearby Lake Nakuru in Kenya killed more than 20,000 lesser flamingos. Fertilizer runoff near Andean lakes is also a concern.

Hunting and egg collecting

Chilean flamingos are listed as Near Threatened partly because their eggs have been harvested and the birds hunted, along with the loss of their habitat.

Disturbance and hazards

Boats, low aircraft, drones and crowds can scare a colony off its nests. Flamingos are slow to switch to new nesting sites, and parents will abandon an egg if a site stops being safe. Power lines near wetlands can also be a collision risk.

Why slow breeding makes it worse

Flamingos usually raise one chick per pair each year, and for lesser flamingos the right conditions for a successful breeding season can take a decade or more to line up. If a lake is polluted, flooded or dry in a key year, a colony may produce almost no young, and the loss can take many years to recover.

What makes a flamingo a flamingo

Every odd feature of this bird is built for life in shallow, salty water.

Pink by diet
Flamingos hatch grey or white. Pigments called carotenoids in algae and brine shrimp slowly turn their feathers pink, which takes around two years.
A beak that filters
They feed with their heads upside down, straining tiny food from water and mud through plates along the beak.
Built for salty water
Lesser flamingos can drink very salty, alkaline water because glands in their heads clear out the extra minerals.
One-legged stance
Standing on one leg is thought to save body heat in cold water.
Life in crowds
They nest in colonies that can number in the thousands, and they do best in large groups.
Long lives
Lesser flamingos live 20 to 40 years in the wild, and some flamingos in zoos reach about 50.

From egg to flock

Flamingo parents work as a team. Here is how a chick starts life.

  1. Build the nest

    Both parents pull mud toward their feet with their beaks to build a mound that lifts the egg above flood water. In places without mud, they use stones.

  2. One egg

    The female lays a single chalky white egg, and both parents take turns sitting on it for 27 to 31 days.

  3. Crop milk

    The chick hatches covered in grey down and is fed crop milk, a red liquid high in fat and protein that both parents make in their throats.

  4. Join the crèche

    After about 5 to 12 days, the chick leaves the nest and joins a crèche, a group of chicks watched over by a few adults. Parents find their own chick by its call.

  5. Learn to filter

    After several weeks the chick can filter-feed. Its straight bill gradually curves, and its feathers turn pink over about two years.

Record holders

Rarest

The Andean flamingo is the rarest and the only one with yellow legs.

Most numerous

The lesser flamingo, with millions of birds, is the most common.

Highest

James’s flamingos feed at lakes up to about 16,000 feet above sea level.

Back from “extinct”

James’s flamingo was thought extinct until a population was found in 1956.

Meet the six

Greater flamingo

Africa, Europe and Asia · Least Concern

The largest flamingo. It feeds in shallow lagoons and salt lakes and is the species most people see in southern Europe.

American flamingo

Caribbean and nearby coasts · Least Concern

The most vividly orange-red flamingo. It lives in the Caribbean and nearby coasts, including the Yucatán and the Galápagos.

Chilean flamingo

South America · Near Threatened

Pale pink with grey legs and pink joints. It lives in highland lakes, lowland wetlands and coasts. This is the species at Woodland Park Zoo.

Lesser flamingo

Africa and South Asia · Near Threatened

The smallest flamingo and the most numerous. It feeds on algae in alkaline soda lakes, and about three quarters of the world’s birds nest at Lake Natron.

James’s flamingo

High Andes · Near Threatened

A small flamingo of high-altitude salt lakes in Peru, Chile, Bolivia and Argentina. It prefers shallow water and was rediscovered in 1956.

Andean flamingo

High Andes · Vulnerable

The rarest flamingo, and the only one with yellow legs. It lives on remote Andean salt lakes that are exposed to mining and drought.

Status key: Vulnerable means a species is considered threatened, Near Threatened means it could become threatened soon, and Least Concern means it is not currently at risk.
